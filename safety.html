Safety

Is eye surgery in Turkey safe? An honest answer.

It can be, when the right things are in place: a qualified eye surgeon at a licensed hospital, a full exam before any treatment, time to decide, and someone checking your eyes after you fly home. Every eye operation carries some risk, wherever it is done, and travelling adds a few of its own. Here is how treatment in Turkey is regulated, what the extra risks are and what we do about each one.

How it is regulated

How eye treatment in Turkey is regulated, in plain terms.

Turkey has its own rules for treating international patients, set by the Ministry of Health. They are not the same as UK or EU rules, so it helps to know who checks what.

Hospitals

Licensed by the Ministry of Health

Private hospitals in Turkey are licensed by the Ministry of Health. Hospitals that treat international patients also need a separate health tourism authorisation.

Going abroad

What makes treatment abroad riskier, and what we do about it.

Going abroad does not change how an operation is done. It changes what happens around it: how much time you have to decide, who you can ask, and who checks your eyes afterwards.

The risk

Booking in a hurry

The UK Royal College of Ophthalmologists recommends time to think before surgery. You get a written plan, then a video call with your surgeon at least 7 days before you fly.

Being treated on an online quote

Risks

The main risks of each treatment, in plain terms.

Every eye operation carries some risk, in Turkey or anywhere else. These are the main ones for each family of treatments. Each treatment page explains them in more detail, and your surgeon goes through yours in writing before you book.

Laser eye surgery

SMILE Pro, Femto-LASIK, Trans-PRK
  • Common, temporaryDry eyes

    Common in the first months and usually settles with lubricating drops. Your eyes are tested for dryness before treatment.

  • Common, temporaryHalos and glare at night

    Rings around lights are normal at first and usually fade over the following months.

  • Common, temporaryA sore, slower first week (Trans-PRK)

    Surface laser is more uncomfortable for the first 3–4 days, and there is a small risk of haze while the surface heals.

  • UncommonUnder- or over-correction

    A few people need a small top-up treatment once their eyes have settled.

  • RareFlap problems, infection or inflammation

    Flap problems apply to Femto-LASIK only. Infection and inflammation are treated with drops when caught early, which is why the next-day check matters.

  • Very rareWeakening of the cornea (ectasia)

    Your scans before treatment screen for the risk, and you are not treated if it is raised.

EVO ICL

A lens placed inside the eye, in front of your own
  • UncommonRaised eye pressure

    Most likely in the first hours and days, which is why your pressure is checked after surgery and the next day.

  • RareCataract

    A clouding of your own lens can develop over time. It can be treated with cataract surgery.

  • Rare, seriousInfection inside the eye

    The risk of any surgery inside the eye. Drops, hygiene and early checks are how it is prevented and caught.

  • Possible, over timeChanges to the inner layer of the cornea

    The lens stays in your eye, so the cells lining the inside of the cornea, your eye pressure and the lens position are checked once a year.

EVO ICL risks

Lens replacement and cataract surgery

Your own lens is replaced with an artificial one
  • Common, temporaryHalos and glare with trifocal lenses

    Common at first and usually fade over time. A small number of people find them bothersome for longer. If you drive at night for work, ask about EDOF lenses.

  • Possible, laterClouding behind the new lens

    Months or years later, vision can cloud again. A quick laser treatment, which an eye clinic near you can do, clears it.

  • UncommonRetinal detachment

    The risk is higher if you are young and short-sighted, which is why lens replacement is not usually advised for short-sighted people under 50. Learn the warning signs below.

  • Rare, seriousInfection inside the eye

    The risk of any surgery inside the eye. Drops and early checks are how it is prevented and caught.

Keratoconus cross-linking (CXL)

Strengthens the cornea to slow keratoconus
  • Common, temporaryPain and light sensitivity

    The surface of the cornea is removed and regrows under a bandage lens, so the first days are sore.

  • PossibleHaze

    A light haze can appear in the cornea while it heals. It usually fades.

  • RareInfection

    Most likely while the surface heals under the bandage lens, which is why you are checked before you fly.

  • PossibleKeratoconus keeps progressing

    In some people the condition carries on changing. Scans at 1, 3, 6 and 12 months show whether it has stopped.

Cross-linking risks

Not everyone is suitable for eye surgery. If your exam shows a higher risk, your surgeon tells you, even if it means no treatment.

After surgery

Warning signs that need urgent care.

Mild scratchiness, watering, light sensitivity and halos at night are common in the first days and weeks. The signs below are different: get them checked the same day, wherever you are.

Get urgent care if you notice

  • Pain that gets worse instead of easing, especially after the first day
  • Vision getting worse, or a sudden drop in vision in one eye
  • New flashes of light, a shower of floaters, or a dark shadow or curtain across your vision
  • Redness, swelling or a sticky discharge that is increasing
  • A severe eye ache with headache, feeling sick or vomiting
  • A knock or injury to the treated eye

What to do

  1. Go to an eye casualty or A&E straight away

    Do not wait for your next video check. In the UK, call 999 in an emergency or NHS 111 if you are unsure. In the EU, call 112.

  2. Show them your surgery report

    It is in English and says what was done and which lens was used. Keep a copy on your phone.

  3. Then message your coordinator

    So your surgeon's team in Istanbul knows, and can share your records with the doctor treating you.

Questions

Safety questions, answered.

Is eye surgery in Turkey safe?

It can be. Laser and lens surgery are established operations, and Turkish hospitals can use the same lasers and lenses as UK clinics; your written plan names the ones used for you. The extra risks come from travelling: less time to decide, language, and follow-up once you are home. Check who your surgeon is, which hospital treats you, and who looks after you afterwards.

Are Turkish eye surgeons qualified?

Eye surgeons in Turkey complete specialist training in ophthalmology and are registered with the Ministry of Health. The title Op. Dr. means a specialist surgeon. Each surgeon's profile shows their qualifications, and you speak to yours on video before you book.

What happens if something goes wrong after I get home?

For anything urgent, go to your local eye casualty or A&E straight away, then tell your coordinator. Our written complication policy says who treats you, who pays and how travel is arranged if you need to come back. Enhancement and complication policy

Will the NHS help if I have a problem?

The NHS gives emergency care to anyone who needs it. It may not provide planned follow-up or further treatment for surgery you chose to have abroad, which is why your follow-up is planned with your surgeon's team before you leave.

Does travel insurance cover treatment abroad?

Usually not. Most standard travel insurance does not cover planned medical treatment. Look for a policy that covers travel for treatment, and check what it says about complications.

Is it safe to fly after eye surgery?

After laser and lens surgery, usually yes, once your surgeon has checked your eyes. That is why each stay ends with a final check before you fly.

Do you arrange eye colour change surgery?

No. We do not arrange cosmetic eye colour change, such as keratopigmentation or artificial iris implants. The American Academy of Ophthalmology has warned against these procedures because they put healthy sight at risk.
