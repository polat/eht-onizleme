How it works

Your natural lens out, a clear artificial lens in.

From your mid-forties, the lens inside your eye stiffens and can no longer focus up close. Lens replacement swaps it for an artificial lens designed to focus at more than one distance.

  1. Measurements decide your lens

    On day 1, scans measure the length and curve of your eye (biometry) and check your retina (OCT). Your surgeon uses them to choose the lens and its power.

  2. Your surgeon removes the natural lens through a small opening

    Under numbing drops, the lens is gently broken up and removed. The folded artificial lens opens in the same natural pocket, called the capsule.

  3. One eye at a time, a day or two apart

    Vision is often blurry at first and improves over the following days. Near and middle vision keep sharpening for weeks as your brain gets used to the new lens.

Is it for me?

Who lens replacement suits, and who it does not.

Your surgeon decides after measuring your eyes and checking your retina. These are the usual limits, so you know where you stand before you send anything.

Usually a good fit if you

  • Are around 45 or over and need glasses for reading, distance or both
  • Are long-sighted: lens replacement is often the main option after your mid-forties
  • Want to rely less on glasses for everyday things like your phone, a computer and driving
  • Have a healthy retina and no other major eye disease, checked on your scans
  • Can accept some halos around lights at night while your eyes adapt, if you choose a trifocal

Something else may suit you better if you

  • Are under 40: your own lens still focuses up close, so laser or EVO ICL usually comes first
  • Are very short-sighted and under 50: lens surgery carries a higher retinal risk at your age, so EVO ICL is usually advised
  • Already have a cataract: the operation is the same, see cataract surgery
  • Drive a lot at night or for work: ask about an EDOF lens instead of a trifocal
  • Have glaucoma, macular degeneration, diabetic eye disease or a past retinal detachment: a trifocal may not suit, and your surgeon needs your records first

Lens types

Trifocal, EDOF or monofocal: which lens?

The operation is the same. The lens decides how you see afterwards. A trifocal aims for near, middle and far. An EDOF lens gives a smoother range with less reading vision. A monofocal focuses at one distance, so most people wear glasses for reading.

Recovery

What the first weeks feel like.

Recovery is gradual: each eye settles over days, and your brain adjusts to the new lens over weeks. This is what is typical. Your surgeon gives you your own written plan.

  1. First few hours

    Blurry, bright and a little gritty

    Rest at the hotel. Your pupil is still wide from the drops, so light feels bright. Sunglasses help.

  2. Next day

    Clearer, though still settling

    Your first eye is checked before the second is treated. Vision can stay hazy for a few days.

    Watching TVShort walksNo rubbing
  3. First week

    Drops, care and flying home

    Use your drops on time and keep soap and water out of your eyes. Flying is fine once your surgeon confirms it.

    Flying homeReading and screensNo swimmingNo eye make-up
  4. Weeks 1–4

    Back to most of everyday life

    Desk work, driving and exercise return as your vision and checks allow. Your surgeon tells you when each one is safe for you.

  5. Months 1–3

    Your brain adapts to the lens

    Near and middle vision often keep improving, and halos around lights usually become less noticeable.

  6. Months or years later

    A haze behind the lens can be cleared

    If your vision slowly clouds again, a quick laser called YAG capsulotomy clears it, usually near home.

Risks

Risks and side effects, explained plainly.

Lens replacement is an operation inside the eye, so it carries some risk. Your surgeon goes through yours in writing, in your language, before you book.

Common, usually temporary

Halos and glare at night

Rings around lights are common with trifocal lenses, especially at first. For most people they become less noticeable as the brain adapts. If you drive a lot at night, ask about EDOF.

Common, temporary

Gritty, dry or blurry first days

Normal while the eye heals. Lubricating drops help, and vision usually clears over the first days and weeks.

Uncommon

A small leftover prescription

Some people still need glasses for certain tasks. Your surgeon will tell you the options, such as glasses or a small laser top-up.

Can develop later

Haze behind the lens

Called posterior capsule opacification. The thin capsule holding your lens can cloud over months or years later. A quick laser clears it, usually near home.

Rare

Retinal detachment

The retina can come away from the back of the eye. The risk is higher in younger, very short-sighted eyes, which is why they are usually steered to EVO ICL. It needs urgent treatment, so you get a written plan for the warning signs.

Rare

Infection or inflammation

Infection inside the eye is rare but serious. Antibiotic drops and your checks in Istanbul are there to catch any problem early.

Your surgeon will tell you if your exam shows a higher risk, even if it means no treatment.

Questions

Lens replacement questions, answered.

How much does lens replacement cost in Turkey?

With Eye Health Turkey, lens replacement with a trifocal lens costs from £1,450 per eye, or £2,900 for both eyes. That includes your exam, surgery, lens, hotel for the nights in your plan, transfers and aftercare checks. UK clinics publish trifocal lens replacement prices of £2,750 to £4,988 per eye.

Am I too young for lens replacement?

It is usually for people around 45 and over, when the natural lens has started to lose its close-up focus. Under 40, laser or EVO ICL usually comes first. If you are very short-sighted and under 50, EVO ICL is usually advised, because lens surgery carries a higher retinal risk at that age.

Trifocal or EDOF: which is better?

Neither is better for everyone. A trifocal is designed for near, middle and far, but halos around lights at night are common at first. An EDOF lens gives a smoother range with fewer halos and a little less reading vision, so it often suits people who drive a lot at night. You choose with your surgeon on your video call.

Will I still need glasses?

The aim is to rely on glasses much less. No one can promise you will never need them: some people still use glasses for certain tasks. Your surgeon will tell you what is realistic for your eyes.

Are both eyes done on the same day?

No. Each eye is treated separately, usually a day or two apart, so your surgeon can check the first eye before treating the second. Your surgeon decides the exact timing.

Does lens replacement hurt?

Numbing drops are used, and most people feel light pressure rather than pain. Afterwards your eye can feel gritty and look slightly red for a few days.

How long do I stay, and when can I fly home?

Usually 4–6 nights. You fly home after your final check, once your surgeon confirms you are fit to fly.

Can I get a cataract after lens replacement?

A cataract forms in the natural lens, so it cannot come back once that lens has been replaced. Months or years later, a different haze can form behind the new lens. A quick laser treatment clears it, usually near home.

Will the halos at night go away?

For most people they become less noticeable over the first months as the brain adapts to the lens. If night driving matters a lot to you, tell your surgeon before you choose a lens.
